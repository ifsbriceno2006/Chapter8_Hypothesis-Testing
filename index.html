Introduction to Hypothesis Testing (Gravetter & Wallnau)

🎯 The big picture

A hypothesis test uses sample data to decide whether a treatment really has an effect on a population, or whether the sample just looks different by chance. It combines three tools you already have: z-scores, probability, and the distribution of sample means (Chapter 7).

The five steps (used for the rest of the course)

  1. State the hypotheses (H0 and H1) and choose α.
  2. Locate the critical region using α and the unit normal table.
  3. Compute the test statistic (here, z) from the sample.
  4. Make a decision: reject H0 or fail to reject H0.
  5. State the conclusion in research-report style: “statistically significant” (or not), the obtained z, p < α or p > α, and Cohen’s d when needed.

📝 Step 5 template

Significant: “The treatment had a statistically significant effect on [variable], z = 2.25, p < .05, d = 0.38.”
Not significant: “The treatment did not have a statistically significant effect on [variable], z = 0.75, p > .05.”

p < α means z is in the critical region (reject H0). p > α means it is not (fail to reject). Add Cohen’s d when the result is significant, to show how large the effect is.

Key formulas

z = (M − μ) / σM    σM = σ/√n
Cohen’s d = mean difference / σ    Power = 1 − β

Decision outcomes

H0 true (no effect)H0 false (effect exists)
Reject H0Type I error (probability α)Correct (power = 1 − β)
Fail to reject H0CorrectType II error (probability β)

⚠️ Common mistakes

  • Writing hypotheses about the sample mean (M). They are always about the population (μ).
  • Saying “H0 is proven true” after failing to reject. The result is only inconclusive.
  • Using σ instead of σM in the z formula.
  • Putting the critical region in the wrong tail for a directional test.
  • Treating “significant” as “big.” Report effect size too.
  • Stopping at “reject H0” without writing the Step 5 conclusion.

8.1 The logic of hypothesis testing

The setup: we know the population before treatment. We want to know what the population mean would be after treatment, but we cannot treat everyone. So we treat a sample and compare it with what untreated samples look like. We assume the treatment, if it works, adds or subtracts a constant, so the shape and σ stay the same.

Null hypothesis (H0): the treatment has no effect.
Alternative hypothesis (H1): the treatment does have an effect. Both are about population parameters, and exactly one can be true.

Alpha level (α): the probability that defines “very unlikely” (usually .05, .01 or .001). Critical region: the extreme sample means, in the tails of the distribution if H0 is true. A sample mean there means we reject H0. For a two-tailed test: α = .05 gives z = ±1.96; α = .01 gives ±2.58; α = .001 gives ±3.30.

✏️ Example 8.1 (worked): red shirts and tips

Waitresses’ tips from male customers average μ = 15.8% (σ = 2.4, roughly normal). Researchers have waitresses wear red and record n = 36 tips.

Step 1: hypotheses and αH0: μred = 15.8 (no effect). H1: μred ≠ 15.8. Set α = .05.
Step 2: critical regionTwo-tailed, α = .05: reject if z is beyond ±1.96. The standard error is σM = 2.4/√36 = 0.4.
Step 3: compute z (if M = 16.7)z = (16.7 − 15.8)/0.4 = 2.25
Step 4: make a decision2.25 is beyond 1.96, so we reject H0.
Step 5: state the conclusion“Wearing a red shirt had a statistically significant effect on tips, z = 2.25, p < .05, d = 0.38.” (d = 0.9/2.4 = 0.38, reported because the result is significant.)
What if M = 16.1 instead?Step 3: z = 0.3/0.4 = 0.75, not in the critical region. Step 4: fail to reject H0. Step 5: “The red shirt did not have a statistically significant effect on tips, z = 0.75, p > .05.” (Cohen’s d is not needed when the result is not significant.)

✏️ Example 8.2: try first

Normal population, μ = 40, σ = 8. After treatment, a sample of n = 16 has M = 45. Is there a significant effect with α = .05 (two-tailed)?

Show solutionσM = 8/4 = 2; z = 5/2 = 2.50. Step 4: this is beyond ±1.96, so reject H0. Step 5: “The treatment had a statistically significant effect, z = 2.50, p < .05, d = 0.63.”

⚖️ A courtroom analogy

H0 is “innocent until proven guilty.” Enough evidence means reject H0 (guilty). Not enough evidence means fail to reject, which is not the same as proving innocence. Likewise, failing to reject H0 does not prove there is no effect.

z as a ratio: z = actual difference between M and the hypothesised μ ÷ the difference expected by chance (σM). A z of 3.00 means the difference is 3 times larger than chance alone would produce.

8.2 Uncertainty and errors in hypothesis testing

A sample gives only partial information, so any decision could be wrong. There are two ways to be wrong. Start with the story, then the formal definitions.

🐺 A simple story: the shepherd and the wolf

A shepherd watches the sheep. The village rule (H0) is “there is no wolf; everything is normal.” The shepherd looks for clues (the sample data) and must choose: shout “WOLF!” (reject H0) or stay quiet (fail to reject H0). A shepherd can’t see everything, so sometimes he gets it wrong.

No wolf (H0 true)A wolf is really there (H0 false)
Shout “WOLF!” (reject H0)😱 Type I: a false alarm. It was a dog’s shadow and everyone ran outside for nothing.✅ Correct: the wolf is caught.
Stay quiet (fail to reject H0)✅ Correct: a calm night.😴 Type II: a miss. The wolf sneaks in and nobody was warned.
  • Type I = crying wolf when there is no wolf. In research: saying “it works!” when it doesn’t.
  • Type II = sleeping while the wolf arrives. In research: saying “no proof” when it really does work.
  • α is how jumpy the shepherd is. A tiny α means he only shouts when he is very sure: fewer false alarms, but he may miss a quiet wolf.

The same idea in other places:

Smoke alarm (H0: no fire)Courtroom (H0: innocent)Research (H0: no effect)
Type IRings for burnt toastConvict an innocent personClaim a treatment works when it doesn’t
Type IISilent during a real fireFree a guilty personMiss a treatment that really works

Type I error

Rejecting a true null hypothesis: concluding there is an effect when there isn’t one. It happens when chance gives you an extreme sample. It is a serious error because it leads to false reports. P(Type I error) = α, which the researcher controls.

Type II error

Failing to reject a false null hypothesis: missing a real effect, often because the effect is small. It is usually less serious. Its probability, β, has no single value because it depends on factors like effect size and sample size.

Choosing alpha: a trade-off

A smaller α lowers the risk of a Type I error, but pushes the critical boundaries farther out (±1.96, ±2.58, ±3.30 for .05, .01, .001), so more evidence is needed to reject H0. An extremely tiny α would make rejection nearly impossible. The largest acceptable value is α = .05.

🔍 A 2-question trick to name the error

  1. What did the researcher decide? If they rejected H0, the only possible mistake is Type I. If they failed to reject, the only possible mistake is Type II.
  2. What is really true? If the decision matches reality, it is correct. If it doesn’t, it is the error from question 1.

You can never make both errors in the same test, because you either reject H0 or you don’t.

8.3 More about hypothesis tests

📰 Step 5: state the conclusion (reporting results)

Example: “Wearing a red shirt had a statistically significant effect on tips, z = 2.25, p < .05, d = 0.38.”
Not significant: “…did not have a statistically significant effect…, z = 0.75, p > .05.”

  • Significant means H0 was rejected: the result is very unlikely if there is no effect.
  • z = 2.25 is the test statistic value.
  • d = 0.38 is Cohen’s d, added when the result is significant to show the size of the effect.
  • p < .05 means the result is in the critical region (and the chance of a Type I error is under .05). Not significant is reported as p > .05.
  • Software gives an exact p (for example, p = .0244). It must still be below .05 to be significant.

✏️ What changes the z-score? (worked)

Start with the red-shirt study: μ = 15.8, M = 16.7, σ = 2.4, n = 36, so z = 2.25 (significant).

More variability, σ = 5.45.4/6 = 0.91.00Fail to reject

Larger mean difference → larger z. Larger σ → smaller z. Larger n → larger z. Experiment below.

✅ Assumptions for the z-test

  • Random sampling so the sample represents the population.
  • Independent observations: one score does not influence another (for example, siblings in the sample would break this; so would sampling without replacement from a small group).
  • σ is unchanged by the treatment (the treatment adds a constant).
  • Normal sampling distribution: the population is normal or n ≥ about 30.

8.4 Directional (one-tailed) hypothesis tests

When there is a strong, pre-stated prediction of direction, put it into the hypotheses. Two things change: (1) H0 and H1 state a direction, and (2) the whole critical region sits in one tail. Everything else is the same.

Tip: write H1 first. If the treatment is predicted to increase scores: H1: μ > 15.8 and H0: μ ≤ 15.8, with the critical region in the right tail. For a decrease, use the left tail. At α = .05 the one-tailed boundary is z = 1.65 (compared with ±1.96).

✏️ Example 8.3 (worked): red shirt should increase tips

μ = 15.8, σ = 2.4, n = 36, M = 16.5.

Step 1: hypothesesH1: μ > 15.8. H0: μ ≤ 15.8. α = .05.
Step 2: critical regionAll 5% in the right tail: z > 1.65. σM = 0.4.
Step 3: zz = (16.5 − 15.8)/0.4 = 1.75
Step 4: make a decision1.75 > 1.65, so we reject H0.
Step 5: state the conclusion“Wearing a red shirt produced a statistically significant increase in tips, z = 1.75, p < .05, one tailed, d = 0.29.” (d = 0.7/2.4.)

✏️ Example 8.4: same data, two-tailed test

With the two-tailed boundaries ±1.96, the same z = 1.75 is not in the critical region, so we fail to reject H0 (Step 4). Conclusion (Step 5): the red shirt did not have a statistically significant effect, z = 1.75, p > .05. A one-tailed test rejects H0 for smaller differences, but only in the predicted direction.

⚠️ Use one-tailed tests carefully

Use one only when the direction was predicted before the study and is strongly justified. Use a two-tailed test when there is no clear expectation or when theories compete. If a two-tailed test fails, never switch to a one-tailed test on the same data to rescue significance.

8.5 Concerns about hypothesis testing: effect size

Two limitations

  1. A test tells you the data are unlikely if H0 is true. It does not give the probability that H0 is true.
  2. Significant does not mean large. The test compares the difference with the standard error, so with a big sample even a tiny effect can be significant.

μ = 50, σ = 10, M = 51.

📏 Cohen’s d

d = mean difference / standard deviation = (M − μ) / σ

It measures the size of the effect in standard deviations and is not affected by sample size. Report it as a positive number. Benchmarks: 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large. In Example 8.5 both tests give d = 1/10 = 0.10.

Distributions overlap even for a large effect, so d = 1.00 can still look modest in a picture. It means the treatment shifted the mean by a full standard deviation.

✏️ Demonstration 8.1 and 8.2 (worked)

μ = 65, σ = 15, n = 25, M = 70 after special reading training. Two-tailed, α = .05.

Hypothesis test (five steps)1. H0: μ = 65; H1: μ ≠ 65; α = .05. 2. Critical region: z beyond ±1.96. 3. σM = 15/5 = 3, z = 5/3 = 1.67. 4. Not beyond ±1.96, so fail to reject H0. 5. “The special training did not have a statistically significant effect on test scores, z = 1.67, p > .05.”
Effect size (optional here)d = 5/15 = 0.33, between the small (0.2) and medium (0.5) benchmarks; the textbook calls it a medium effect.

8.6 Statistical power

Power is the probability that a test correctly rejects a false H0, so it detects a real effect. Power = 1 − β, so a power of 70% means β = 30%. Researchers compute power before the study to see if it is likely to succeed.

To compute power: (1) find the critical boundary on the null distribution (μ ± zcrit·σM); (2) centre a second distribution of sample means at μ + effect; (3) find the proportion of that treated distribution beyond the boundary.

✏️ Example 8.6 (worked)

μ = 80, σ = 10, n = 25, expected effect +8 points, two-tailed α = .05.

Step 1: standard errorσM = 10/5 = 2.
Step 2: critical boundaryM = 80 + 1.96(2) = 83.92.
Step 3: treated distribution (centred at 88)z = (83.92 − 88)/2 = −2.04. Proportion above: p(z > −2.04) = 0.9793.
ConclusionPower ≈ 97.93%: the study is almost sure to detect an 8-point effect. With only a 4-point effect (centre 84), z = −0.04 and power = 0.5160.

✏️ Example 8.7: try first

Same study but n = 4. Find the power.

Show solutionσM = 5; boundary M = 80 + 1.96(5) = 89.8; z = (89.8 − 88)/5 = 0.36; power = p(z > 0.36) = 0.3594 (35.94%).

What increases power?

  • A larger treatment effect (bigger effect size).
  • A larger sample (smaller σM). Also a smaller σ.
  • A larger alpha (for example .05 instead of .01).
  • A one-tailed test (when the effect is in the predicted direction).
